# Copy the brain's principles, not its wiring

*Research report, 12 October 2026. Synthesised from five research notes in `research/research_notes/Brain replica and AI research/`. Labels: **(vendor claim)** means the developer's own figure with no independent check; **(unverified)** means a single secondary source; **(snippet only)** means only a search summary was read, not the full page.*

Neither Claude nor anyone else can build an exact replica of a human brain in October 2026. The data that would define one does not exist. The biggest complete synapse-level wiring map is a fruit fly's (~166,700 neurons). Mammal maps cover only about 1 mm³ of tissue. Forty years after the worm's 302-neuron wiring diagram, no model reproduces the worm's full behaviour from it. Hardware is just as far off. The largest neuromorphic machines hold 1-2 billion simplified neurons at about 2 kW. Scaled to 86 billion neurons, that is roughly 4,000-10,000x the brain's 20 W, with about a thousand times fewer synapses. Nobody knows the brain's learning rule either, though a February 2026 *Nature* study gave the first direct evidence that cortex sends a neuron-specific error signal to each neuron. What is achievable is narrower and still worth doing: brain-*inspired* systems that borrow the principles behind the brain's efficiency (sparse, event-driven activity; memory stored next to compute; local and fast plasticity; strong built-in priors; interactive data), and tested at fly, mouse-circuit and benchmark scale. For research, the record is consistent. AI reliably narrows a search space (candidate drugs, algorithms, hypotheses, literature). It is unreliable at judging novelty, at interpretation and at citations, and every validated "AI discovery" so far needed humans plus a lab assay or a formal checker. Use Claude as a tireless analyst and coder inside a preregistered, falsifiable workflow, not as an oracle.

## A replica needs data that has not been collected yet

"Exact replica" requires three things: the complete wiring, the state of every synapse and cell, and a way to check the copy behaves like the original. As of October 2026 none of the three exists for any mammal. The largest finished connectomes are insect-scale. FlyWire mapped the adult female fly brain at 139,255 neurons and 50M+ synapses in 2024 ([NIH](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/complete-wiring-map-adult-fruit-fly-brain)). The male fly's whole central nervous system, at **166,700 neurons**, was published in *Cell* on 3 September 2026 ([Sci.News](https://www.sci.news/biology/complete-fruit-fly-connectome-15053.html); [Janelia](https://www.janelia.org/news/researchers-reveal-connectome-of-the-male-fruit-fly-central-nervous-system)). For mammals, the largest dense reconstruction is MICrONS, **1 mm³ of mouse visual cortex with ~120,000 neurons and 523M synapses** ([MICrONS Explorer](https://www.microns-explorer.org/cortical-mm3)). The human H01 sample covered 1 mm³ of temporal cortex and produced 1.4 PB of images ([Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adk4858)). The human brain is about 1.2 million mm³, so EM imaging at H01's rate implies roughly an exabyte of raw data before anyone traces a neuron. That figure is my arithmetic, not a published estimate.

Imaging is no longer the binding cost; proofreading is. Wellcome's 2023 review set a whole mouse connectome as a **10-15 year goal** and put manual proofreading at roughly **100,000 person-years** ([Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/insights/reports/scaling-connectomics)). Via E11 Bio, Wellcome's cost breakdown gives **$7-21B** for proofreading against ~$300M for imaging ([E11 Bio](https://e11.bio/blog/roadmap)). Cost per reconstructed neuron has dropped from ~$16,500 for the original worm to ~$100 in recent zebrafish projects ([State of Brain Emulation 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15745v2)). The bet for reaching the mouse is expansion microscopy. LICONN (*Nature*, June 2025) expands tissue ~16-fold, reaches below 20 nm resolution on standard light microscopes, and adds molecular labels ([ISTA](https://ista.ac.at/en/news/piecing-together-the-brain-puzzle/)). E11 Bio's PRISM adds protein barcodes so the segmentation can proofread itself. E11 targets a full optical mouse connectome in about five years, at least 100x cheaper **(vendor claim; target, not result)** ([E11 Bio](https://e11.bio/blog/roadmap)). Its October 2025 pilot covers a patch of hippocampus, not a whole brain ([E11 PRISM](https://www.e11.bio/blog/prism)). The independent estimate is still "within 10 to 15 years" for a mouse ([MIT McGovern, Dec 2025](https://mcgovern.mit.edu/2025/12/15/all-the-connections/)).

Even a perfect wiring map would not be enough. The worm is the control experiment. **40-50% of synaptic connections differ between genetically identical worms** ([SoBE 2025](https://brainemulation.mxschons.com/)), so "the" connectome does not define a worm. The best worm model, BAAIWorm (*Nature Computational Science*, Dec 2024), reproduces basic locomotion only after adding ion channels, morphologies, electrophysiology and calcium imaging, and validated just 5 neurons by patch clamp ([PKU](https://nbic.pku.edu.cn/en/Achievements/d57de79476bf4b279d32e0c285da6405.htm); [SoBE 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15745)). No animal has single-neuron recordings of ≥95% of its brain. Larval zebrafish reaches ~80% and the worm ~50% ([SoBE 2025](https://brainemulation.mxschons.com/)), so even small-brain models cannot be fully fitted or checked. The fly is the partial success. Shiu et al. (*Nature* 2024) built a leaky integrate-and-fire model of the whole fly brain from FlyWire, and its predictions about which neurons drive feeding were confirmed by optogenetics in real flies ([Nature](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v634y2024i8032d10.1038_s41586-024-07763-9.html)). It still leaves out gap junctions, baseline firing and neuromodulation. Eon Systems' 2026 demo connected that model to a simulated body that walked, groomed and fed. That is a striking result, but it has no peer-reviewed paper, and its reported accuracy figures vary between outlets **(vendor claim)** ([ISPR](https://ispr.info/2026/04/21/researchers-upload-model-of-flys-brain-to-matrix-let-it-control-virtual-body/)).

Coverage often confuses two different kinds of "brain simulation". The first is exact wiring with simplified neurons (fly, worm). The second is huge size with statistical wiring. Fudan's Digital Twin Brain ran **86 billion spiking neurons and 47.8 trillion synapses on 14,012 GPUs**. Its wiring came from MRI tractography, not synapses, and it ran **65-119x slower than real time** ([arXiv 2308.01241](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.01241)). The Fugaku mouse cortex (SC25, Nov 2025) simulated ~9 million detailed neurons and 26 billion synapses from atlas statistics, with no real-time factor reported ([UEC](https://www.uec.ac.jp/eng/news/announcement/2025/20251118_7309.html)). No system is both connectome-exact and biophysically complete at mammalian scale. The 2025 State of Brain Emulation report, written by 45+ contributors, judges that organisms under 1M neurons could be emulated "within the decade, at a cost in the low $100Ms", and notes that **fewer than 500 people worldwide** work on brain emulation ([SoBE site](https://brainemulation.mxschons.com/)). I found no credible published date for a human replica. Capturing one person's synaptic strengths, molecular make-up and dynamic state with today's destructive, anatomy-only methods has no known path, and there is no test for whether a copy matches the person.

| Scale | Wiring mapped? | Working emulation? | Status Oct 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worm (302 neurons) | Yes, since 1986; varies 40-50% between individuals | Basic locomotion only, with lots of extra data | Established |
| Fly (~139k-167k) | Yes (brain 2024, male CNS 2026) | Some specific circuits predicted and confirmed; embodied demo is a vendor claim | Partial |
| Mouse (~70M) | 1 mm³ only | No; statistical cortex model on Fugaku | Connectome 2029 (vendor target) to late 2030s |
| Human (~86B) | 1 mm³ sample | Statistical model only, ~100x slower than real time | No credible date |

## Twenty watts is a communication budget, and silicon is thousands of times off

The brain's efficiency comes from communicating little, not from cheap arithmetic. Levy & Calvert (PNAS 2021) estimate that of ~4.94 W of ATP in human cortex, communication (gray-matter signalling plus white matter) takes **~71% and computation only ~2%** ([PMC8106317](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8106317)). Their headline 35:1 ratio depends on counting all white-matter cost as communication. Lennie (2003) showed that spikes are expensive enough to limit concurrent strong activity to **possibly fewer than 1% of cortical neurons** ([Lennie 2003](https://www2.bcs.rochester.edu/sites/plennie/pdfs/Lennie03a.pdf)). Silicon has the same bottleneck. GPUs spend most of their energy moving weights between memory and compute, and Epoch AI's inference estimate assumes only **~10% GPU utilisation** ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use)). The brain avoids this by storing weights at the synapse and spending energy only when a spike arrives. "Brain FLOPS per watt" comparisons are unreliable: estimates of the brain's operations per second span roughly 10^13-10^17 ([Open Philanthropy](https://www.openphilanthropy.org/research/how-much-computational-power-does-it-take-to-match-the-human-brain/)), and a synaptic event is not a multiply-accumulate.

On the AI side, a typical chatbot text query costs roughly **0.24-0.3 Wh**. Google's 0.24 Wh is a measurement of its own fleet **(vendor measurement)**, and Epoch's 0.3 Wh is an estimate. A 100k-token long-context query costs ~40 Wh ([Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/how-much-energy-does-chatgpt-use); [Ritchie summary](https://www.sustainabilitybynumbers.com/p/ai-footprint-august-2025)). The brain uses 20 Wh per hour, so one ordinary query equals about a minute of brain energy and a long-context query about two hours. Training is where the gap is huge. No frontier lab publishes training energy. Secondary estimates for GPT-4 range from ~1.75 GWh to ~50 GWh ([RISE](https://www.ri.se/en/news/blog/generative-ai-does-not-run-on-thin-air)), against roughly 12 MWh for a 20 W brain over 70 years. That puts training at somewhere between ~140 and ~4,000 human lifetimes of brain energy (my arithmetic). The size of the gap is uncertain; its existence is not.

Neuromorphic hardware has reached about 1-2% of human neuron counts, but at kilowatts and with far fewer synapses. Nearly every efficiency figure in the field comes from the vendor:

Scaling Hala Point to 86 billion neurons would take ~75 systems and ~195 kW at peak. Scaling Darwin Monkey would take ~43 systems and ~86 kW. Both are roughly 4,000-10,000x the brain's 20 W, before accounting for the synapse shortfall or the much simpler neuron models (my arithmetic from vendor figures). The two power numbers are not comparable either, since one is a maximum and the other is "typical". The only serious attempt at independent comparison is **NeuroBench** (*Nature Communications*, Feb 2025), a shared benchmark harness for algorithms and systems ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/c4d9e92cc504488f84821bfaf348306a)); its leaderboard numbers were not retrieved for this report. Neuromorphic chips clearly win today in one place: always-on sensing at hundreds of microwatts. For dense LLM inference, the best efficiency result comes from a non-spiking near-memory chip (NorthPole), which says memory placement matters more than spikes.

Speed follows the same pattern. The ~77,000-neuron Potjans-Diesmann cortical microcircuit runs in about real time on a single RTX 2080 Ti ([Frontiers 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computational-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fncom.2021.627620/pdf)). On a CPU with NEST it needed ~63 s per biological second ([arXiv 2007.14236](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.14236)). Point-neuron models of about 10^5 neurons are a solved real-time problem for a hobbyist GPU. Detailed models at mouse-cortex scale need an exascale machine and are not reported as real time. Biological computing (Cortical Labs CL1 at $35,000 a unit, FinalSpark's remote organoids) uses real neurons, so the per-neuron energy really is brain-like. But the systems hold at most ~10^4-10^6 neurons, live up to ~6 months, and have shown only simple closed-loop tasks such as Pong. "A million times less power" is an unmeasured company claim that leaves out incubation and recording electronics ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/a-breakthrough-in-computing-cortical-labs-cl1-is-the-first-living-biocomputer-and-costs-almost-the-same-as-apples-best-failure); [FinalSpark via ANSA](https://www.ansa.it/sito/notizie/economia/business_wire/2024/05/15/finalspark-launches-the-first-remote-research-platform-using-human-neurons_5fcb882a-72de-4bcb-993d-493194fc30dc.html)).

The practical conclusion for the energy thread: software alone can recover the sparsity win on any hardware (skipping zeros, event-driven updates, mixture-of-experts routing). The owner's own measurement recorded ~3.6x fewer operations at 7% activity with matching weights (`learning/efficient-brain-like-learning.md`). The memory-movement win needs hardware with weights stored next to compute. Matching 20 W needs both, and neither is close at brain scale.

## Nobody knows the learning rule, but cortex now looks like it sends per-neuron errors

No single cortical learning rule is established. Hebbian/STDP plasticity and dopamine-gated "three-factor" plasticity are established mechanisms. How cortex assigns credit across many layers is the open question. The strongest new evidence is Francioni, Harnett et al., *Nature*, 25 Feb 2026. Mice learned a brain-computer-interface task, and the apical dendrites of individual layer-5 neurons carried error signals whose **sign depended on each neuron's own causal role**. These signals predicted how each neuron changed, and a single shared-signal model did not fit. Silencing them by activating NDNF+ layer-1 interneurons **disrupted learning** ([PMC13112360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13112360/); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10190-7)). The authors call it the first biological evidence of a vectorized solution to credit assignment. The signal looks like an error *derivative*, which fits backprop-like and target-propagation models alike. It is one study, in one area (retrosplenial cortex), on an artificial task, and it needs replication. Still, it weakens "scalar dopamine is all cortex has" and supports proposals such as error-neuron microcircuits ([PMC13089762](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13089762/)) and dendritic backprop-through-time ([Nat Commun 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-66666-z)).

In engineering terms, local learning rules still trail backprop badly. A June 2026 paper found the best Forward-Forward variant reached **49.4% on ImageNet-100 against 75.6%** for a backprop-trained ResNet-50, with the gap growing as classes are added. It also used *more* memory, which undercuts FF's main selling point ([arXiv 2606.06539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06539)). The best reported ImageNet-1K result for a local rule is ~25.7% top-1 (snippet only) ([arXiv 2605.00082](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00082)). Biological plausibility and engineering competitiveness remain separate goals.

The sample-efficiency gap is real and large. Toddlers produce grammatical sentences after ~10M words, and a preteen has heard ~100M. Llama 3.1 trained on **15 trillion tokens** ([MIT Technology Review, Aug 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/24/1141740/kids-machines-language-learning/)), a gap of **3-6 orders of magnitude** ([Frank 2023](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/colareadinggroup/readings/Frank2023_ChildrenLLMs.pdf)). BabyLM, the challenge that trains models on child-scale data, shows the gap is not about data order. Curricula disappointed, and new objectives and architectures did best. Training compute did not fully predict score ([BabyLM 2025 findings](https://aclanthology.org/2025.babylm-main.28)). Deep nets also show a second failure that brains do not: under continual training they gradually **lose the ability to learn at all**. Dohare et al. (*Nature* 2024) fixed this with "continual backprop", which reinitialises a small fraction of low-utility units, an analogue of synaptic turnover or neurogenesis ([Nature](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07711-7)).

The 2025-2026 "brain-inspired" models mostly borrow principles on top of backprop, and independent checks shrink their claims. HRM claimed 41% on ARC-AGI-1. ARC Prize verified **32%**, and found the **outer refinement loop**, not the brain-like hierarchy, drove the result ([ARC Prize](https://arcprize.org/blog/hrm-analysis)). Its successor TRM (7M parameters, 2 layers) reports 44.6% on ARC-AGI-1 and 7.8% on ARC-AGI-2 ([arXiv 2510.04871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871)). Pathway's BDH uses Hebbian fast weights as working memory and claims GPT-2-level language performance **(vendor claim)** ([arXiv 2509.26507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26507)). SpikingBrain claims >100x faster time-to-first-token on long contexts **(vendor claim; spikes simulated on GPUs)** ([arXiv 2509.05276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.05276v1)). VERSES' AXIOM "learns 39 times faster than DreamerV3" was validated by a firm VERSES paid, not by peer review **(vendor claim)** ([press release](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2025/06/02/3091981/0/en/VERSES-Digital-Brain-Beats-Google-s-Top-AI-At-Gameworld-10k-Atari-Challenge.html)). The Thousand Brains Project's Monty shows fast continual learning from Hebbian-like binding in sensorimotor object recognition, and calls itself "nascent" ([arXiv 2507.04494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04494)). Zador's genomic-bottleneck argument explains the pattern. The genome cannot specify synapses one by one, so evolution passes compressed wiring rules that act as priors ([Nat Commun 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-11786-6)). On current evidence, brain-like sample efficiency is more likely to come from good priors, good credit assignment and interactive, grounded data than from an exact copy of biophysics. That is a working hypothesis, not a settled finding.

## AI research tools narrow the search; humans and assays decide

Every validated AI-for-science result follows the same shape. The AI proposes a short list from a huge space, and something outside the AI confirms it. AlphaEvolve found a 4x4 complex-valued matrix multiplication using **48 scalar multiplications**, beating Strassen's 49 for that setting ([arXiv 2604.11504](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.11504)). Gemini Deep Think and an OpenAI model each scored 35/42 at IMO 2025, gold level ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/21/gemini-imo/)). Stanford's Virtual Lab agents designed 92 nanobodies, and wet-lab tests confirmed two with improved binding to new SARS-CoV-2 variants (*Nature* 2025) ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/article/10.1038/s41586-025-09442-9)). Google's Co-Scientist proposed 30 leukemia drug candidates. Oncologists cut the list to 5 for lab testing, 3 showed some positive effect and 1 looked promising. FutureHouse's Robin got 2 promising hits from its top 5 for macular degeneration. Both papers appeared in *Nature* in 2026, and both depended on humans for framing and selection ([Verspoor, The Conversation](https://phys.org/news/2026-05-ai-scientists-reveal-fundamental-limits.html)). The neuroscience-specific result is BrainBench. LLMs picked the real result over an altered one in neuroscience abstracts **~81% of the time, against 63% for 171 experts** ([Nature Human Behaviour 2025](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/large-language-models-surpass-human-experts-in-predicting-neurosc/)). That shows strong pattern-matching of the literature, which helps prioritise experiments. It can also bias a model against genuinely surprising findings, which is exactly the kind a gap-filling researcher is after (hypothesis, untested).

The failures are just as consistent, and they cluster around novelty, interpretation and citations. In October 2025, OpenAI researchers announced that GPT-5 had "solved" ~10 open Erdős problems. It had found existing solutions in the literature, and the post was withdrawn ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/19/openais-embarrassing-math/)). Edison's Kosmos, by its own evaluation, was **85.5% accurate on data-analysis statements but only 57.9% on interpretation**, and 3 of its 7 "discoveries" reproduced findings humans had already made **(vendor self-evaluation)** ([arXiv 2511.02824](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02824)). An independent evaluation of Sakana's AI Scientist reported failed experiments and established ideas labelled as novel; the specific 42% failure figure comes from a secondary summary ([arXiv 2502.14297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14297)). Sakana's one workshop-accepted paper did not meet Sakana's own main-conference bar ([arXiv 2504.08066](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.08066)). Citation fabrication is now measurable at scale. NeurIPS 2025 accepted 53 papers containing 100 fabricated citations ([arXiv 2602.05930](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05930v1)). An audit of 111M references estimates at least **146,932 hallucinated citations in 2025** ([arXiv 2605.07723](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.07723)). When asked to produce citations, 13 LLMs fabricated **14-95%** of them ([GhostCite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06718)).

Claude's documented science record is infrastructure and speed-ups, not discoveries. Anthropic offers PubMed and other connectors through Claude for Life Sciences (Oct 2025) and an AI for Science API-credit programme (May 2025) ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-life-sciences); [Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11199177-anthropics-ai-for-science-program)). Its January 2026 case studies report a GWAS done in ~20 minutes in Biomni and a gene-cluster interpretation in which Claude flagged a pathway other models dismissed **(vendor claim)** ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/accelerating-scientific-research)). Reports of a "Claude Science" workspace (June 2026) and an Aug 2026 expansion of credits for scientists are **unverified** single secondary sources ([letsdatascience](https://letsdatascience.com/news/anthropic-launches-claude-science-and-drug-program-9ddae733); [Natural 20](https://natural20.beehiiv.com/p/anthropic-supercharges-claude-for-scientific-discovery)). I found no peer-reviewed paper whose central finding is credited to Claude, and no case of an AI agent autonomously generating and validating a new neuroscience finding.

## A falsifiable workflow for a lab-less researcher

The workflow follows from the evidence. Let Claude do what it is measurably good at: search, code, analysis, simulation and generating candidates. Never let it be the judge of novelty, of interpretation or of a reference. Every claim the repo publishes should come from a test that could have failed, run on public data or a public benchmark, with a preregistered prediction. Publishers and funders agree on the rules. AI cannot be an author, its use must be disclosed in the Methods, and humans stay accountable ([COPE](https://publicationethics.org/guidance/cope-position/authorship-and-ai-tools); [EU living guidelines](https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en)).

In practice, each session runs the same loop. Claude searches PubMed (official connector) and arXiv/bioRxiv (a third-party paper-search MCP, unaudited) for prior art first ([paper-search-mcp](https://mcpservers.org/en/servers/openags/paper-search-mcp)). Every reference is resolved through a DOI, PMID or arXiv ID lookup before it enters a topic file, and a bare title from a model is never accepted. A read-only verifier subagent checks citations and claims separately from the agent that wrote them. The prediction and the success threshold go on OSF (timestamped) before the analysis is run, not only in a GitHub issue. Claude writes the analysis code, a human reads it, and the run pins seeds and package versions. Claude's interpretation of results is treated as a draft hypothesis, because interpretation is where AI systems score worst. Restricted human data (HCP restricted tier, NDA) never goes to a cloud model; the HCP terms forbid re-identification attempts ([HCP terms](https://humanconnectome.org/study/hcp-young-adult/data-use-terms)).

Each of the three research threads maps to open data, a benchmark that can say "no", and a realistic contribution:

For publishing, the main constraint changed in January 2026. arXiv no longer endorses new submitters on institutional email alone; they need a prior accepted paper in the field or a personal endorsement ([arXiv blog](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026)). The realistic route is a double-blind workshop paper first (BabyLM accepts archival 8-page papers through OpenReview), then arXiv. Public benchmark contributions such as Brain-Score plugins, and citizen science such as FlyWire proofreading, which is open to anyone ([FlyWire](https://join.flywire.ai)), build a citable record and a network of possible endorsers. Algonauts 2025 shows outsiders can compete. MedARC, a volunteer collective, placed fourth among 260+ teams ([arXiv 2508.10784](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10784)). Before any paper, check the AI for Science credit programme; it favours life sciences, and a neural-data analysis project plausibly fits.

## Conclusion

The question "how do I make Claude build an exact brain replica" turns out to contain three separate problems, and each has its own bottleneck. Wiring is blocked by proofreading cost and by individual variation. Learning is blocked by an unidentified credit-assignment mechanism, which the 2026 dendrite result has narrowed. Energy is blocked by data movement, and brain and chip pay for it in the same way. None of the three bottlenecks is compute that Claude could supply. All three are bottlenecked by *measurements and tests*, so the most valuable thing an independent researcher can add is not a bigger model but a sharper falsification. Examples: how much wiring precision a fly emulation really needs; whether a per-neuron error signal closes the gap between local rules and backprop; how many synaptic operations per correct answer a sparse learner spends on a standard benchmark.

The research-tool evidence points the same way. Systems that produced validated results were attached to something that could say "no" (an assay, a proof checker, a held-out benchmark), and systems that failed were judging their own novelty. For this repo, Claude should sit inside a loop whose final step is a public benchmark or dataset that the owner did not write and Claude cannot persuade. Built that way, a one-person project can contribute real, citable pieces to all three threads within a year, even though an exact human replica will not exist within any timeframe the evidence supports.
