## van de Ven 2020: Brain-inspired replay for continual learning with artificial neural networks
- **Cited for:** H093 - replaying generated (not stored) past experience prevents forgetting.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-17866-2 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can generative replay (GR) without stored data scale beyond toy continual-learning problems (many tasks, natural images), and how good must the replay be?
- **Method / model:** Classifier plus a separate VAE generator (following Shin et al.); at each step a minibatch of current data (128) is mixed with 128 generated, self-labelled samples, so the replay cost does not grow with the number of tasks. Compared with EWC, SI, XdG, LwF, plain fine-tuning (None) and joint training. The new "brain-inspired replay" (BI-R) replays hidden (not pixel) representations generated by the network's own context-modulated feedback connections, plus gating by class, inhibition of the previous generator and "replay-through-feedback".
- **Experiments:** Split MNIST in task-incremental (Task-IL) and class-incremental (Class-IL) scenarios (20 repetitions); permuted MNIST with 100 permutations (Domain-IL, 5 reps); split CIFAR-100 in 10 tasks (10 reps); ablations of each BI-R component.
- **Key results:**
  - Class-IL on split MNIST: EWC and SI "dramatically fail"; only GR prevents catastrophic forgetting without stored data. In Task-IL all compared methods work.
  - Replay can be small or poor: one replayed sample per 128 current samples still beats all non-replay methods in Class-IL; a VAE with only 10 hidden units (blurry samples) only moderately hurts. A control that re-initialises the net each task removes this robustness.
  - Standard GR does not scale: on permuted MNIST it degrades after about 15 tasks, and it fails on CIFAR-100 even in Task-IL; BI-R beats SI after 100 tasks and reaches state of the art for CIFAR-100 Class-IL without stored data (BI-R + SI is best).
- **Limitations / caveats:** Needs an extra generative model; performance measure is average accuracy over tasks so far; semi-/unsupervised settings and non-image modalities are untested; the paper notes that explicit-storage methods (iCaRL, experience replay) were previously the only ones that worked on CIFAR Class-IL.
- **Bearing on H093:** Supports. Generative replay beats no replay and regularisation on Class-IL. It does not itself run a head-to-head against exact (stored) replay, which is Shin 2017's comparison.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Split-digit (2 classes at a time) numpy MLP: sequential fine-tune vs. replay of samples drawn from a small per-class Gaussian/PCA generator vs. none; accuracy over all classes after the last task.

## Shin 2017: Continual learning with deep generative replay
- **Cited for:** H093 - generative vs exact replay vs none.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can a generator (instead of a stored buffer) supply old data so a solver keeps old tasks while learning new ones, inspired by hippocampus/neocortex complementary learning systems?
- **Method / model:** A "scholar" = generator G (WGAN-GP) + solver S. For a new task, train on new real data mixed with (x', y') where x' is sampled from the previous generator and y' is the previous solver's response; the generator is trained on new data plus the old generator's samples, so it reproduces the cumulative input distribution.
- **Experiments:** Permuted-MNIST task sequence; MNIST -> SVHN and SVHN -> MNIST domain pairs; split MNIST in 5 tasks of 2 digits each (the class-incremental setting). Variants named in the text: GR, ER (exact replay of real past data with old-solver labels, the upper bound), Noise (Gaussian noise inputs with old-solver labels) and None (naive training).
- **Key results:**
  - Chain of five solvers trained only through generated data: 98.81, 98.64, 98.58, 98.53, 98.56 % MNIST test accuracy.
  - Permuted MNIST: GR keeps earlier tasks; None forgets catastrophically; Noise replay does not help; accuracy rises the closer the replayed inputs are to real data (figures, no table values).
  - Split MNIST (2 classes at a time): only ER and GR approach the upper bound; Noise (right labels, meaningless inputs) and None fail.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Efficacy "heavily depends on the quality of the generator"; some loss when the generator must model SVHN; figures give no exact numbers beyond Table 1; MNIST-scale only.
- **Bearing on H093:** Supports, and matches the row's falsifier almost exactly (GR vs ER vs Noise vs None). Replay of meaningless inputs gives no protection, so "generated" must be good enough.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Same as van de Ven but with four arms: exact replay, generated replay, noise-input replay, none, on split digits.

## Tononi 2014: Sleep and the price of plasticity
- **Cited for:** H094 - sleep scales synapses down, keeping the strongest and raising signal-to-noise.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.12.025 · **Basis:** full text (web; first ~100k characters read, the end of the review was not retrieved)
- **Question:** Why does the brain need sleep? Proposes the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (SHY): learning during waking potentiates synapses net, sleep renormalises them.
- **Method / model:** A review/hypothesis paper. Waking learning raises net synaptic strength (cost: energy and supply demand, lower signal-to-noise, saturated learning, the "plasticity-selectivity dilemma"); during sleep, slow-wave activity and low neuromodulator levels drive activity-dependent "down-selection" that weakens less-used synapses and protects strongly activated ones. Simulations cited suggest this improves S/N, gist extraction and integration of new with old memories.
- **Experiments (evidence reviewed):** rodent GluA1 AMPAR levels reported 30-40 % higher after wake than sleep; evoked-response slope in rat cortex rises with waking and falls with sleep, tracking slow-wave activity; human TMS-evoked slope rises over 18 h awake and returns after sleep; mEPSC frequency/amplitude higher after wake; Drosophila VS1 spines grow after enriched wake and shrink only with sleep; mouse spine gain in wake and loss in sleep; sleep-loss and slow-wave manipulation studies in human encoding.
- **Key results:**
  - Net synaptic strength is higher after wake than after sleep across molecular, electrophysiological and structural markers.
  - Local slow-wave activity rises after learning in the engaged area and drops after disuse.
  - Sleep deprivation impairs next-day encoding; boosting slow oscillations in a nap improved it.
- **Limitations / caveats:** The authors list that markers can reflect neuromodulator-driven excitability or sleep inertia; most animal studies only enforced wakefulness without a learning task; whether replay instructs potentiation (active-consolidation view) is contested; REM's role and inhibitory synapses unresolved; direct in vivo tests are called for.
- **Bearing on H094:** Supports as a hypothesis, not a proven mechanism. The row's "keeps the strongest" is the down-selection claim (proportional or threshold scaling is not specified in this text). Evidence is correlational; do not state it as established.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Train a net on tasks A then B with small Hebbian-style growth; insert a multiplicative downscale (and optional prune of the weakest) step between tasks; compare task-A retention and a signal-to-noise measure vs. no downscale (issue #84).

## Frey 1997: Synaptic tagging and long-term potentiation
- **Cited for:** H095 - only tagged synapses capture the proteins needed for lasting change.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/385533a0 · **Basis:** abstract only (from the publisher page; full text paywalled)
- **Question:** How can late-phase LTP be input-specific when protein synthesis happens mainly in the cell body?
- **Method / model:** Proposes that LTP induction sets a short-lived, protein-synthesis-independent "synaptic tag" at the potentiated synapse that sequesters the relevant proteins.
- **Experiments:** Hippocampal slice recordings with two inputs to the same neuron population: weak tetanus (normally early LTP only), or repeated tetanus under protein-synthesis inhibitors, tested after repeated tetanisation of the other input.
- **Key results:**
  - Weak stimulation, or strong stimulation under inhibitors, gives protein-synthesis-dependent late LTP if repeated tetanisation was applied at another input to the same population.
  - The tag decays in under three hours.
  - Persistence of LTP depends on prior activity of the neuron, not only on local events at induction.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Not assessable from the abstract (details such as intervals, group sizes and controls were not read).
- **Bearing on H095:** Supports. The abstract directly describes tag plus capture of proteins. The model's use for selective retention in networks is the repo's extension.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** A weight with a fast tag trace (decay ~ minutes) and a slow consolidation variable that only grows when tag and a global "protein" signal coincide; compare retention of tagged vs untagged updates against a plain rule.

## Fusi 2005: Cascade models of synaptically stored memories
- **Cited for:** H096 - synapses with hidden multi-timescale states balance learning and forgetting.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2005.02.001 · **Basis:** abstract only (PubMed abstract; sources.json has none)
- **Question:** How can synapses store memories of ongoing experience (needs plasticity) while retaining them against later changes (needs stability)?
- **Method / model:** Each synapse is a cascade of states with different plasticity levels, linked by metaplastic transitions; compared with switch-like models whose transition probability is either high (good storage, poor retention) or low (the reverse).
- **Experiments:** Not described in the abstract (theoretical/simulation study).
- **Key results:**
  - The cascade "combines high levels of memory storage with long retention times and significantly outperforms alternative models".
  - Suggests memory storage needs synapses with multiple states over a wide range of timescales.
- **Limitations / caveats:** No numbers could be taken from the abstract. Benna & Fusi (below, full text) state that the cascade's memory lifetime scales at most as the square root of the number of synapses.
- **Bearing on H096:** Supports (the stability-plasticity framing is explicit).
- **Reproduce in this repo:** See Benna 2016; run simple bounded synapse vs a 4-8 level cascade, one random +/-1 memory per step, measure memory SNR vs age.

## Benna 2016: Computational principles of synaptic memory consolidation
- **Cited for:** H096 - hidden multi-timescale synaptic variables, same memory lifetime for cascade vs simple synapse is the falsifier.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.4401 · **Basis:** full text (web; the arXiv preprint 1507.07580, titled "Computational principles of biological memory", not the journal version)
- **Question:** What synaptic model preserves the most memories under continual random overwriting?
- **Method / model:** Each synapse has m coupled variables u_1..u_m (u_1 is the weight read by the circuit) with exponentially growing capacities and time constants, coupled bidirectionally like communicating vessels (a chain of beakers with growing size and shrinking tubes); a modification enters u_1 and diffuses to slower variables and back. Memory is tracked as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of one tracked pattern against later random uncorrelated patterns; lifetime = time until SNR falls below order one.
- **Experiments:** Simulated populations of N synapses (e.g. N = 5.4 x 10^9 in one figure) with m from 4 to 10 variables, one new uncorrelated memory per time step; also bounded-variable versions, parameter perturbations, and spacing-effect/metaplasticity demonstrations.
- **Key results:**
  - SNR decays roughly as 1/sqrt(t) over an interval that grows exponentially with m, then exponentially; lifetime grows exponentially with m up to order N, and linearly with N at fixed m.
  - With the longest timescale adjusted, lifetime scales as N / log N, versus at most sqrt(N) for the earlier cascade model; for N about 10^14 the paper estimates a capacity gain of almost 10^7.
  - Robust to parameter perturbations; reproduces delayed expression of modifications, metaplasticity and spacing effects.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Assumes random uncorrelated memories and an optimal readout; the authors call the uncorrelated-memory assumption strong but standard. The preprint text may differ from the journal version.
- **Bearing on H096:** Supports. Multi-timescale hidden state raises lifetime far above a single variable, and the cascade vs simple comparison is already in the paper. The H096 row's Benna citation is the journal title; the preprint carries a different title.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** N synapses with a chain of m=1,3,6 coupled variables (capacity doubling each level), store random +/-1 patterns one per step, plot SNR of the first pattern vs time; expect a longer 1/sqrt(t) range as m grows.

## Kirkpatrick 2017: Overcoming catastrophic forgetting in neural networks
- **Cited for:** H097 - synapses resist change in proportion to importance (EWC, Fisher-based).
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1611835114 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can a network learn tasks sequentially if weights important to old tasks are made harder to change?
- **Method / model:** Elastic weight consolidation (EWC): after task A, add a quadratic penalty on task B of the form (lambda/2) * sum_i F_i (theta_i - theta*_A,i)^2 where F is the diagonal of the Fisher information matrix at the task A solution (Laplace approximation to the posterior). Each weight is pulled back in proportion to its importance. Motivated by synaptic consolidation in neuroscience.
- **Experiments:** Permuted MNIST in sequence (EWC vs SGD, L2 and SGD+dropout); Fisher overlap between tasks across layers; Atari 2600: 10 games chosen from a pool of 19, 4 seeds per set, deep Q-network with a task-recognition model; EWC penalty activated after 20 million frames in a game; test of Fisher quality by perturbing weights along the inverse Fisher vs uniform noise.
- **Key results:**
  - On permuted MNIST only EWC keeps old tasks; L2 either blocks learning the new task or forgets; dropout alone does not fix forgetting.
  - Fisher overlap between tasks is higher in later layers and lower in early layers when tasks differ more, so the net allocates separate capacity as needed.
  - On Atari EWC keeps performance on revisited games while SGD forgets.
- **Limitations / caveats:** The Fisher is computed offline at the end of each task (not a local online synaptic estimate); the Laplace/diagonal approximation under-estimates uncertainty (the authors say weight perturbations suggest over-confidence); capacity is fixed.
- **Bearing on H097:** Partly supports. EWC is the importance-weighted resistance, but the Fisher is a global gradient-based statistic. The "synapses estimate their own importance" part is closer to Zenke's online per-synapse path integral.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Two-task linear/MLP net: compare diagonal-Fisher penalty vs the local running |grad * dw| importance (SI-style) vs none; see Zenke below.

## Zenke 2017: Continual learning through synaptic intelligence
- **Cited for:** H097 - local importance estimate vs EWC with a Fisher matrix.
- **Link:** https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/zenke17a.html · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can each synapse track its own importance online, locally, during training and use it to avoid forgetting?
- **Method / model:** Synaptic intelligence (SI). Each synapse k accumulates a path integral omega_k = sum over steps of (-gradient_k * change in parameter_k) during task training, normalised by the squared total parameter change plus a damping term xi, and summed over past tasks to give Omega_k. New-task loss adds c * sum_k Omega_k (theta_k - theta_k_ref)^2. Needs only quantities available locally at each synapse (gradient, update).
- **Experiments:** Split MNIST (5 tasks of 2 digits, multi-head, 2 hidden layers of 256 units), permuted MNIST (10 tasks, compared with EWC numbers replotted from Kirkpatrick), split CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 (CNN; 60 epochs on CIFAR-10 then 5 CIFAR-100 tasks of 10 classes), c in 1e-3..0.1.
- **Key results:**
  - Consolidation (c > 0) preserves task-1 accuracy over later tasks; without it (c = 0) accuracy collapses.
  - On permuted MNIST results are "comparable to the results reported with EWC", close to training on all data jointly; SGD and SGD+dropout are far worse.
  - On CIFAR the consolidated net keeps similar validation accuracy across tasks, and was always better than without consolidation except on the last task.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Authors state only that the penalty is "similar to EWC" and the two give similar performance, possibly because the importance measures correlate; they make no claim that biological synapses do this; the path integral is noisy and needs damping.
- **Bearing on H097:** Partly supports. A local importance estimate matches EWC, it does not beat or lose by a lot, so the falsifier ("much worse") is consistent with the paper. The row cites both papers for "synapses estimate their own importance"; only Zenke does so.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Split-MNIST-style numpy MLP: SI importance (running sum of -g*dw divided by (total change)^2 + xi) vs diagonal Fisher penalty at equal c; compare average accuracy.

## Nader 2000: Fear memories require protein synthesis in the amygdala for reconsolidation after retrieval
- **Cited for:** H098 - retrieval makes a memory labile again, enabling updates.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/35021052 · **Basis:** abstract only (publisher page; full text paywalled)
- **Question:** Is a consolidated fear memory labile again after it is retrieved?
- **Method / model:** Rats; infusion of the protein-synthesis inhibitor anisomycin into the lateral and basal amygdala after memory reactivation.
- **Experiments:** Infusion shortly after reactivation at 1 or 14 days after conditioning; control infusion without reactivation; infusion delayed 6 hours after reactivation.
- **Key results:**
  - Anisomycin after reactivation produced amnesia on later tests, whether reactivation was at 1 or 14 days.
  - The same treatment without reactivation left memory intact.
  - A 6-hour delay produced no amnesia, so reconsolidation has a time window.
- **Limitations / caveats:** The finding is about disrupting a memory; it does not show that new information gets integrated into the old trace.
- **Bearing on H098:** Partly supports. It shows retrieval renders memory labile and dependent on protein synthesis, but "which lets old knowledge be updated" is an interpretation beyond this paper (the paper shows loss, not updating).
- **Reproduce in this repo:** A store with a "labile" flag set on read for a window; allow overwrite/merge only while labile; compare adaptation after a rule change vs an append-only store.

## Ramsauer 2021: Hopfield networks is all you need
- **Cited for:** H099 - hippocampus works like attention over stored patterns (modern Hopfield ~ transformer attention).
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02217 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can a continuous-state Hopfield network with an exponential energy store exponentially many patterns, retrieve in one update, and be used as a deep-learning layer?
- **Method / model:** Energy built from log-sum-exp of query-pattern similarities; the update rule xi_new = X softmax(beta X^T xi) is the transformer's attention (queries, keys, values). Three kinds of fixed points: global average, metastable states over subsets, single patterns. Hopfield layers: HopfieldPooling, Hopfield and HopfieldLayer.
- **Experiments:** Multiple-instance learning (Tiger, Fox, Elephant, UCSB breast cancer), immune repertoire classification (DeepRC, hundreds of thousands of instances; AUC 0.832 +/- 0.022 vs the next-best 0.825), UCI small classification tasks, and drug-design datasets; analysis of transformer/BERT heads (global averaging in early layers, partial averaging in higher layers).
- **Key results:**
  - Storage capacity exponential in the associative-space dimension (e.g. 2^(d/2) for the binary predecessor); retrieval error exponentially small in pattern separation.
  - Equivalence of the update rule to attention.
  - State of the art on three of four MIL problems, on UCI small tasks, and on two drug-design datasets.
- **Limitations / caveats:** The paper contains no mention of the hippocampus (zero occurrences in the text); its claims are about machine learning, not about the brain.
- **Bearing on H099:** Does not test it. The Hopfield = attention equivalence is established here; the hippocampus link rests on Whittington et al. The H099 row's wording "(a modern Hopfield network ≈ transformer attention)" is accurate, but Ramsauer alone should not be cited for "the hippocampus works like attention".
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Store N random patterns; retrieve with a noisy query via one softmax(beta X^T xi) step vs a classical Hopfield outer-product rule; count recall errors vs N.

## Whittington 2022: Relating transformers to models and neural representations of the hippocampal formation
- **Cited for:** H099 - hippocampus as attention over stored patterns.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04035 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Is a transformer with recurrent position encodings mathematically related to the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine (TEM), a neuroscience model of the hippocampal formation, and does it show place and grid cells?
- **Method / model:** Shows that TEM's memory network (Hopfield-like attractor storing conjunctions of path-integrated position g and sensory x, retrieved by attention) is analogous to self-attention with learned recurrent positional encodings; builds TEM-transformer (TEM-t). Also applies the Krotov & Hopfield (2020) two-pool neuron implementation to interpret memory neurons as place cells.
- **Experiments:** Spatial tasks in simulated environments; comparison of TEM-t with the original TEM code on sample efficiency, time per gradient step, and zero-shot accuracy on links not taken before; inspection of learned units for grid-like and place-like tuning, including random remapping across environments.
- **Key results:**
  - TEM-t learns grid-cell-like representations and place-like memory neurons that remap randomly across environments.
  - It is much more sample- and time-efficient than TEM: TEM's authors report up to 50,000 gradient updates for full training, while TEM-t was stopped at 20,000 (figure only, authors say it is not an optimised, fair comparison).
  - TEM-t handles larger problems with many more memories.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Random place-cell remapping is not the full picture (real remapping is non-random); the transformer-hippocampus link is a mathematical analogy and a model, not a test of the biology; speed comparison not claimed fair.
- **Bearing on H099:** Partly supports. It gives the hippocampus link (a hippocampal model equals a transformer), but this is model-to-model equivalence. The H099 falsifier ("Hopfield-memory agent, no one-shot recall advantage") is not tested by either paper.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** A Brain World agent with softmax-attention recall over stored (state, outcome) pairs vs no memory; episodes to criterion on a one-shot cue-recall task.

## Ba 2016: Using fast weights to attend to the recent past
- **Cited for:** H100 - a second, fast-decaying weight set stores recent context.
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06258 · **Basis:** full text
- **Question:** Can an RNN benefit from a third time scale of variables, between activities and slow weights?
- **Method / model:** Fast weight matrix A(t) = lambda * A(t-1) + eta * h(t) h(t)^T (decay, outer-product Hebbian store). The next hidden state is computed over S inner-loop steps: h_{s+1} = f(LN[W h(t) + C x(t) + A(t) h_s]) with layer normalisation; computed without forming A, as a decay-weighted sum of past hidden vectors times their dot product with the current one. Defaults: fast learning rate 0.5, decay 0.95.
- **Experiments:** Associative retrieval (key-value strings, 100k train, 10k validation, 20k test); MNIST with a visual attention model using glimpses; facial expression recognition (multi-level attention); reinforcement-learning agent with memory. Baselines: IRNN, LSTM, associative LSTM (A-LSTM), ConvNet.
- **Key results:**
  - Associative retrieval error with 20/50/100 recurrent units: IRNN 62.11/60.23/0.34 %; LSTM 60.81/1.85/0 %; A-LSTM 60.13/1.62/0 %; fast weights 1.81/0/0 %.
  - Fast weights help most when the number of recurrent units is small and converge faster than the LSTMs.
  - Fast weights improve a glimpse-based MNIST model over LSTM/IRNN (error rates in the paper's Table 2 depend on the glimpse setup).
- **Limitations / caveats:** The gain over LSTM vanishes at 100 units; the ConvNet still wins on the frontal-face data; comparisons in the table are against IRNN/LSTM, not a plain tanh RNN; biological claims are motivational.
- **Bearing on H100:** Supports. A fast, decaying outer-product memory solves associative retrieval where small RNNs fail.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** numpy RNN (50 units) on key-value retrieval with and without A(t) (lambda 0.95, eta 0.5, one inner step with layer norm); compare error.

## Mongillo 2008: Synaptic theory of working memory
- **Cited for:** H102 - working memory held in short-term synaptic changes, not firing.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1150769 · **Basis:** abstract only
- **Question:** Is working memory sustained by persistent reverberating spiking, or by synaptic facilitation?
- **Method / model:** Calcium-mediated synaptic facilitation in recurrent neocortical connections; presynaptic residual calcium is used as a buffer that is loaded, refreshed and read out by spiking activity.
- **Experiments:** Not described in the abstract (network model).
- **Key results:**
  - Long calcium time constants allow a low refresh rate, making the mechanism metabolically efficient and robust.
  - Duration and stability of the memory can be regulated by modulating spontaneous activity.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Not assessable from the abstract; it does not state the silent-memory claim or the distractor result in so many words.
- **Bearing on H102:** Partly supports (the abstract says memory is held by synaptic facilitation with low-rate refreshing). "Silent" and "survives a distractor" are not in the abstract. Verify against the full text before relying on them.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** A recurrent net with short-term facilitation (u, x variables) holding an item with an input pulse, then a distractor pulse and a read-out pulse; compare to a rate-only attractor.

## Wagner 2004: Sleep inspires insight
- **Cited for:** H105 - hidden rules are found after sleep, not after equal time awake.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/nature02223 · **Basis:** abstract only (publisher page; full text paywalled)
- **Question:** Does sleep facilitate insight into a hidden abstract rule?
- **Method / model:** Human behavioural study with a stimulus-response sequence task that can also be improved abruptly after discovering a hidden rule.
- **Experiments:** Initial training, then 8 h of nocturnal sleep, nocturnal wakefulness or daytime wakefulness, then retest; also a group without initial training. (Group sizes were not read.)
- **Key results:**
  - More than twice as many subjects gained insight after sleep as after wakefulness, regardless of time of day.
  - Sleep did not enhance insight without initial training.
  - A slowing of reaction times across sleep was a characteristic antecedent of insight.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Human behaviour only, no mechanism; abstract gives no sample sizes or statistics.
- **Bearing on H105:** Supports the behavioural claim. The mechanism "offline replay" is the repo's model, not shown here (Lewis & Durrant propose it).
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Train on the task, then an offline phase with replay and noise vs an equal number of online steps without; fraction of seeds discovering the hidden rule.

## Sahay 2011: Increasing adult hippocampal neurogenesis is sufficient to improve pattern separation
- **Cited for:** H106 - adult-born neurons keep similar memories apart.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09817 · **Basis:** full text (web)
- **Question:** Does increasing the number of adult-born dentate gyrus neurons, without changing other factors, improve discrimination of similar contexts?
- **Method / model:** Mice with an inducible Nestin-CreERT2 and floxed Bax; tamoxifen deletes Bax in neural stem cells so more adult-born neurons survive; vehicle-injected littermates as controls (about 57.5 % of Dcx+ neurons recombined).
- **Experiments:** Contextual fear discrimination of similar contexts (three versions of the paradigm), single-trial fear conditioning, object recognition, water maze, active place avoidance, extinction, anxiety/depression assays, exercise; LTP recordings in the dentate.
- **Key results:**
  - Dcx+ neurons up about 1.8-fold, BrdU+ surviving cells about 3.6-fold; neurogenesis-dependent LTP enhanced.
  - Treated mice discriminated similar contexts better (treatment effect F(1,18)=6.15, P=0.023); controls needed about four more days to reach the same discrimination; the authors call this "suggestive of enhanced pattern separation".
  - No change in single-trial fear conditioning, object recognition, water-maze learning, extinction, or anxiety tests.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Recombination incomplete and also in stem cells; possible changes in neuronal packing density; one task family; the pattern-separation claim is described as suggestive.
- **Bearing on H106:** Supports (more adult-born neurons, better discrimination of similar contexts). It adds neurons by extra survival, not by adding new units mid-training as in the row's model; the mapping is the repo's.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** Autoencoder/associative net on overlapping inputs; add a few high-plasticity sparse units after the first task vs fixed units; measure interference between similar patterns.

## Dasgupta 2017: A neural algorithm for a fundamental computing problem
- **Cited for:** H107 - sparse random expansion (fly mushroom body) makes one-shot learning and similarity search easy.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aam9868 · **Basis:** abstract only (PubMed; bioRxiv text blocked)
- **Question:** Does the fruit-fly olfactory circuit solve similarity search, and can its strategy improve computer algorithms?
- **Method / model:** The fly circuit is described as a variant of locality-sensitive hashing that uses three computational strategies departing from the traditional approach (the abstract does not list them).
- **Experiments:** Not described in the abstract.
- **Key results:** Similar odors get similar neural activity patterns, so behaviors learned from one odor transfer to a similar odor; the strategies "can be translated to improve" similarity search.

## Masse 2018: Alleviating catastrophic forgetting using context-dependent gating and synaptic stabilization
- **Cited for:** H108 - a different subset of neurons per task stops interference.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1803839115 · **Basis:** full text (arXiv 1802.01569)
- **Question:** Does a task-specific, mostly non-overlapping sparse gating of hidden units, alone or with synaptic stabilisation (SI/EWC), allow learning many tasks in sequence?
- **Method / model:** Context-dependent gating (XdG): for each task a fixed random subset (80 % in the main runs) of hidden units is multiplied by zero, in training and testing, so each task uses a sparse, mostly non-overlapping subnetwork; combined with SI or EWC (an RL-compatible variant of SI used for reinforcement learning).
- **Experiments:** Permuted MNIST (two hidden layers of 2000 units) up to 100 tasks, and up to 500 for an extended run; ImageNet split into 100 tasks; recurrent networks on cognitive tasks trained by supervised and reinforcement learning.
- **Key results:**
  - Ungated net: 98.5 % single-task accuracy, falling to 52.5 % after 10 permutations and 19.1 % after 100.
  - SI alone: 97.0 % after 10 and 82.3 % after 100 permutations; XdG alone: 97.1 % after 10 and 61.4 % after 100.
  - XdG + SI: 95.4 % mean accuracy after 100 tasks (first task 98.2 %); after 500 tasks XdG + SI gets 90.7 % vs 54.9 % for SI alone. Best accuracy with 80-86.7 % of units gated.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Task identity must be supplied at test time; the authors note that XdG alone is only partially effective; gating subsets are random and fixed; far from a learned context mechanism.
- **Bearing on H108:** Supports. A per-task unit subset reduces forgetting, and works best with synaptic stabilisation. Note that XdG alone still falls to 61.4 % at 100 tasks, so "stops interference" overstates it.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** MLP on K permuted digit tasks, with fixed random 80 % unit masks per task vs none, both with and without a SI penalty; average accuracy across tasks.

## Iyer 2022: Avoiding catastrophe: active dendrites enable multi-task learning in dynamic environments
- **Cited for:** H108 - context switches on a different subset of neurons per task.
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fnbot.2022.846219 · **Basis:** full text (arXiv 2201.00042)
- **Question:** Can pyramidal-neuron properties (active dendritic segments, sparsity via k-winner-take-all) give context-specific routing for multi-task RL and continual learning?
- **Method / model:** "Active Dendrites" neurons: a feedforward weighted sum modulated by dendritic segments (each is a weight vector on a context vector; the segment with the maximum response sets a multiplicative gain), followed by kWTA (and sparse weights). Only winning neurons and their selected segments get gradients, so a small context-dependent subnetwork is updated. Context is the task ID in RL, or a prototype vector in continual learning (given during training, or built on the fly by clustering).
- **Experiments:** Meta-World v2 MT10 (ten manipulation tasks, one robot arm) vs an MLP baseline; permuted MNIST continual learning up to 100 tasks, compared with XdG and SI.
- **Key results:**
  - Meta-World MT10: Active Dendrites 87.5 % average end success rate vs 76.6 % for the MLP (the text also gives 95.6 % vs 88.2 % for the best five runs of each).
  - Permuted MNIST: 94.6 % and 81.4 % on 10 and 100 tasks with context given in training; 94.3 % and 76.9 % when the prototype is chosen automatically; with SI, 97.2 % and 91.6 %.
  - Subnetworks that emerge are overlapping but distinct and sparse.
- **Limitations / caveats:** Context is supplied or inferred by prototypes; learned dendritic gating is more complex than random masks, so it is not the same mechanism as H108's "a different subset"; the SI comparison is only in combination with it.
- **Bearing on H108:** Partly supports. Shows a learned, soft per-task subnetwork reduces interference, with overlap allowed. Differs from Masse's random fixed masks, so the row's single description fits Masse more than Iyer.
- **Reproduce in this repo:** As Masse's, plus a variant with a learned gain per segment (max over K segments chosen by context) in place of random masks.
